《10 million users, $125M fundraise, Suno CEO: the future of music is “multiplayer”》

In May 2024, Suno CEO Mikey Shulman did something on someone else's podcast: he entered a prompt on the spot and generated a trap-style song about “unsupervised learning” in a few dozen seconds. The show's opening music was also made with his own product.

At that point, over 10 million people had already generated songs with this AI music tool, and Suno had just closed a $125 million round at a $500 million valuation. On Redpoint Ventures investor Jacob Effron's Unsupervised Learning show, this physics PhD and Kensho veteran (LangChain founder Harrison Chase is his old colleague) talked for nearly an hour about the real data and real missteps behind the viral hit.

Several of his judgments rather overturn first impressions: he deliberately doesn't touch pricing innovation; he thinks “a three-and-a-half-minute pop song indistinguishable from human work” is a boring goal; he believes open source AI is overhyped; and he blames today's AI product pricing, wholesale, on the SaaS playbook from a decade ago.

Two kinds of users, and that Zamboni song

The host asked him to name his favorite Suno work. His answer: an art song in the German art song (Lied) style, sung in Estonian. He doesn't understand Estonian and doesn't normally listen to that genre, but he replayed it about a hundred times, about a fifth of the song's total plays. What moved him was the care the anonymous creator put into crafting the song.

He has another favorite category: songs made with his 3-year-old son. The most famous is one about his son driving a Zamboni (the ice resurfacing truck); three-year-olds in Massachusetts are of course not allowed to drive one, and that's what makes it fun. The host also contributed an example: the producer sent a pile of podcast equipment to his home, boxes piled up, and he made a country song on the spot to complain about it.

Behind these two examples lies his understanding of music: the most enjoyable part of music-making is the process itself, while the entire industry today serves only the final product. In his words, making music with other people is one of the most enjoyable moments of his life, and those songs mark moments that will never come back.

Suno's users split clearly into two kinds. One is casual users, playing what the team calls “a soundtrack for your life”: writing songs when Starbucks gets your name wrong, writing songs when a pile of gear suddenly arrives at your door. Music is one of humanity's oldest ways of telling stories, and these funny moments are worth reliving through song.

The other is power users: with a sound in their heads and a story to tell, they'll happily sink two hours into it. The most confirmatory finding for the team was a large group of people with great musical taste and clear ideas in their heads, who simply never had the tools. Ableton, Pro Tools, Logic, even GarageBand are all good things, but the learning curves are too steep.

There's also a small detail. Suno lets users edit song titles, and they found that users who made the trending page would go back and add their own names to the titles. The team's reaction was to lean in, not to correct: letting users feel proud of their work is a lever worth pressing hard on.

The blank text box is “sterile”

With 10 million users, what most people see on their first visit is still an empty input box. Mikey admits the blank canvas problem isn't truly solved. They ran a Valentine's Day event with good results, because the reason to create was obvious: writing a song for your lover.

From this he derived a product principle: you have to make users realize that making a song needs no reason, just like sending a text or taking a photo needs no reason. Music should be a first-class citizen of expression, standing alongside text and photos.

Future input methods won't stop at text. He listed a stack: hum the melody in your head, tap the table for a beat, describe your current mood, make a mood board from sounds in life. Some already clink glasses, sample the sound, post TikToks of the process, and produce hit songs. His judgment: so many AI tools still rely on text input, which is itself evidence of how early this industry is. Back then it took many years just to figure out how people want to listen to music on their phones.

The real problem with the text box is that it's a “sterile” way to express musical intent. The better way is to sing your heart out, or put together a few photos that move you, or gather the sounds of everyday life to inspire the model.

He added a layer of logic: when the maker enjoys the process, the listener is more easily moved. This is a virtuous cycle, and the product should serve that cycle.

The future of music is multiplayer

This was the direction he mentioned most all episode. Concretely, there are two forms: synchronous, simulating a jam session, I give an idea, you respond, you riff and I take over, completely fluid; asynchronous, I send half a song and you finish it, or I send a musical idea, you modify it and send it back.

His reasoning is simple: music is a conversation. Real-life conversations happen both synchronously and asynchronously, face to face or over Slack, and musical conversation should be the same.

Everyone has taste; that's the ticket into collaborative music-making. You give the lyrics and sounds, I take them, adapt them, extend them, pass them back, and after an hour we may both have had a great time and ended up with a five-minute song we'll replay countless times. Mikey calls himself a mediocre musician, but that doesn't stop him from jamming with friends: “The most fun I've ever had on this planet was jamming with friends. I see no technical hurdle to giving that joy to everyone.”

There's already a prototype. A Twitch streamer with a decent following livestreamed himself making songs with Suno for two hours, and the audience reached the size of a football stadium, with viewers paying small tips to interact with the streamer. Mikey calls it an interactive digital concert. The hosts also mentioned a viral video of Charlie Puth making a beat on Jimmy Fallon's show by hitting a mug with a spoon; the live creation process naturally draws viewers.

He extended the scenario: playing real-time-generated songs at sports events, with fans and players joining in, and even making a song with your favorite athlete. “That would feel great, I promise.”

On competitive landscape he's very open: the market is big enough, professional artist tools and background music (think how many YouTube videos need background music) are all big businesses, but Suno focuses on ordinary people. “I want my mom playing with this. My mom isn't a musician.”

He blames SaaS pricing on the people from a decade ago

Suno's current pricing is very plain: free songs, paid for heavy users. Mikey said outright that they are deliberately not innovating on business model, because product form is still changing drastically and no one knows how it will ultimately shake out.

Then came the sharpest passage of the episode. He said, the people building and investing in AI companies today are the same group who built and invested in SaaS companies a decade ago, and everyone has almost blindly copied SaaS subscription pricing over. He also joked to the host (himself a VC): “This pot I'll hand to you, and you can pass it to your peers.”

The problem is, generating a song has marginal cost; it's not a SaaS world of near-zero marginal cost. A user who goes wild with a subscription a hundred thousand times means a hefty GPU bill. He guessed that music, text, and video will ultimately each have different pricing models, depending on product and use case.

What about the $125 million? His answer was: scale everywhere. Training bigger models, plus research investment, specialized data, control axes, and talent. Music models don't need as much compute as the biggest text models for now, but the right way to model music hasn't even been worked out; in text, everyone is doing small variants of the same architecture. The principle for deploying capital is one sentence: spend money where they can more quickly pull the future of music they envision closer.

Music has no benchmark: evals rely on people, speed fought for in milliseconds

Model evaluation is much harder in music than in text. Automatic metrics for audio quality exist but are deeply flawed. The company's internal slogan is “aesthetics matter”: soft metrics and subjective judgment can't be dodged, so you need people who truly love music doing the evaluation, because music has no correct answer. Text has reasoning benchmarks; music has nothing comparable.

The real quality test is how much users love the songs, and that splits into two things: the songs themselves must be moving, and the model must produce what the user wanted. The second point, control, is an important metric with no good automatic measurement, so heavy human eval. Mikey even said he's “not sure it will improve in the future.”

The host mentioned that Sourcegraph's CTO said the best eval is shipping and watching user acceptance. Mikey responded that they have double signals: implicit ones are usage after a new model ships and choices between models; explicit ones are a Discord community of about 300,000 people reporting problems directly. Fixing is case-by-case; for example, a version once didn't end songs cleanly, with overly long outros and trailing silence; once diagnosed, the fix wasn't hard. The cliché still holds: the more time you spend looking at your data, the better you understand your model.

North star metrics aren't complicated: how many people are making songs, how many come back day after day, the probability of exhausting the free quota (even without paying, it means those few minutes were enjoyable), and whether it got shared or received. Sharing grows ever more important; this is ultimately a social game. What the model still can't do is iterative editing: the output is wrong, you know exactly where it's wrong, yet there's no way to say “keep it, change X.” BPM (beats per minute) is an axis with objective answers; improvement will be fast.

On speed, he named Spotify as the ceiling: click play and it sounds instantly. Suno also has to generate the song, and the gap is real. His approach: every 100 milliseconds that pass, users are more likely to click away, and the journey of making music breaks off. Engineering-wise they use Transformer autoregressive architecture, streaming to the user while generating, a natural advantage over diffusion models. He stresses users don't care about your model architecture, only whether the song arrived fast and moved them.

Infrastructure-wise, they're picky about where to innovate themselves and where to buy. They are Modal's customers and fans; Mikey specifically declared “I'm not being paid to say this” (host Effron then admitted his firm is a Modal investor). Audio lags behind image and text, so many problems were solved first by the image/text and open source communities; for example, the continuous batching they would have had to write themselves, vLLM open-sourced before they needed it. His advice to peers: be deliberate about where you innovate and where you don't.

Counterintuitive takes: open source overhyped, cover songs are a flash in the pan

The quick-fire round followed the “hot take” format from his old colleague Harrison Chase: 80% true is enough; the point is the nugget of truth inside. Several judgments were quite sharp.

On open source: he thinks open source AI is overhyped. Open source software is great, but open source AI is hard, because compute costs rise with each generation of models, and without a business model it's hard to catch up; the best open source models come from Meta, which has the money. In the debrief the hosts added: open source can handle mature use cases discovered a year ago; the most economically valuable capabilities are always at the closed-source frontier; the two markets will coexist for a long time.

On Discord: he admitted he was completely wrong. They thought they'd stay on Discord a long time (Midjourney is the example), but in November 2023 they launched a web version that didn't even have all of Discord's features, and after five days, 90% of usage migrated to the web. His conclusion: Discord is a messaging platform, unsuited to a complete music experience; what they make is experience, and the model is only the bottom layer. But without Discord at the start there'd be no 300,000-person community, and that community is an underappreciated asset, even crowdsourcing prompt engineering for them. Another failure: the first thing after leaving Kensho was buying a few GPU servers, which now gather dust at home; he completely failed to anticipate the scale of demand.

On artist collaborations: covering Charlie Puth without the artist's explicit authorization is illegal, and he believes such viral spreads are a flash in the pan, far smaller than outsiders imagine in the future of music. His analogy: the first time you used GPT you surely had it write Shakespeare-style sonnets about VC investing; by the third time you found it silly. GPT is still something you use every day, just not for that. Better onboarding is handing users the truly useful scenario the first time, like pasting messy data and getting JSON out directly. The musical version: put the interesting sounds heard while making coffee this morning into it and build a song together.

On the audio wave: GPT-4o's voice mode and ElevenLabs are both impressive, but he believes for a long time to come, audio will remain an “interface” into the LLM; the understanding layer is still done in text; true consolidation into a single big model will take longer than outsiders expect. Host Effron added: cloud vendors all need their own model cards, Snowflake is rumored buying a model company, Databricks, Amazon, Google, Microsoft are all present, which will counterbalance consolidation toward a single model.

On milestones: many people care “when a model can make a three-and-a-half-minute pop song indistinguishable from human studio work.” He thinks this goal is boring, because music has no objective right or wrong; the ceiling is much higher; music is about how it moves you. His personal dream is a Vision Pro app where he plays air guitar in a band, or conducts a symphony, with the music responding to him in real time. “Games are things humans have played for millennia; the video game industry is huge.”

Oh, and if he weren't doing Suno, he'd want to do something unsexy: home plumbing. No telemetry, entirely manual, and the hardware existed 30 years ago. His exact words were “smart sewer pipes.”

What you can use tomorrow

For people building products, this episode contains several directly usable levers:

  • Before pricing, calculate marginal cost. If every user action burns GPU money, copying SaaS subscriptions wholesale means losing more the more you sell. Mikey's approach of “stabilize the product first, touch pricing later” is also a strategy.
  • Millisecond-level latency is a retention lever. Stream what can be streamed, and treat “the user clicks away after 100 milliseconds” as a design constraint, not an optimization item.
  • When automatic metrics fail, build a community that dares to tell the truth. Suno's 300,000-person Discord is both a customer service desk and a free eval team.
  • Give users a “reason to create.” Don't put an empty input box in front of people; learn from that Valentine's Day event: bind creation to an obvious scenario.
  • Let users feel proud. Signable titles, shareable cards, showcase pages; these small designs press on vanity, and vanity is a real driver of retention.

Mikey said at the end of the show that they're hiring in New York and Cambridge, Massachusetts, and also remote worldwide. He said the reason is simple: there's too much to do, and they can't think of it all themselves. As for how big this market really is, his judgment is: the music market is smaller than it should be, and AI can expand it. This game has only played the top of the first inning.
